§ 0

The Experimental Setup

Every superconducting-qubit experiment is fundamentally a microwave measurement. A control tone is synthesised at room temperature, sent down attenuated coax to a dilution refrigerator, scattered off a chip held at millikelvin temperatures, and the reflected (or transmitted) signal is amplified, mixed back to baseband and digitised.

The pieces that matter for calibration are: an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG) producing baseband I and Q envelopes, an IQ mixer driven by a local oscillator (LO) that up-converts those envelopes to GHz, and a separate readout chain that uses a dispersively coupled resonator to interrogate the qubit's state.

Fig. 0.1 — control & readout schematic

The transmon, in a circuit

A transmon is the simplest useful superconducting qubit: a Josephson junction shunted by a large capacitance, so that the charging energy $E_C$ is much smaller than the Josephson energy $E_J$. In this regime the qubit becomes insensitive to charge noise, paid for by reduced anharmonicity.

It is read out by capacitively coupling it (coupling capacitance $C_g$) to a quarter- or half-wavelength superconducting resonator at frequency $\omega_r$.

$$ \hat{H}_{\text{transmon}} = 4 E_C (\hat{n}-n_g)^2 - E_J \cos\hat{\varphi} $$
In the transmon limit ($E_J/E_C \gtrsim 50$) the spectrum is a weakly anharmonic ladder with
$$ \omega_{01} \approx \frac{\sqrt{8 E_J E_C}}{\hbar} - \frac{E_C}{\hbar}, \qquad \alpha \equiv \omega_{12}-\omega_{01} \approx -\frac{E_C}{\hbar}. $$

STEP 01

Readout Resonator Spectroscopy

First we find the resonator. With the qubit unprepared (and effectively decoupled at low probe power), the bare resonator simply reflects everything except at its resonance frequency, where the field builds up and is dissipated.

Procedure

  1. Set readout LO so the up-converted tone sweeps a range of, say, 6.0 – 7.5 GHz in 0.5 MHz steps. Use very low power (well below one photon on average).
  2. For every frequency, send a CW probe through the feedline, demodulate the transmitted I and Q, compute $|S_{21}|$.
  3. Look for a dip (notch geometry) or peak (transmission geometry). Fit a Lorentzian.

What the data looks like

Around the resonance, the transmitted amplitude for a notch-type resonator follows

$$ S_{21}(\omega) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{(\kappa_{\text{ext}}/\kappa)\, e^{i\phi}}{1 + 2 i (\omega - \omega_r)/\kappa}. $$
$\omega_r$ is the resonance frequency, $\kappa = \kappa_{\text{int}} + \kappa_{\text{ext}}$ is the total linewidth (FWHM in angular units), and the loaded quality factor is $Q_L = \omega_r/\kappa$.
Fig. 1.1 — |S₂₁| vs probe frequency
Worked example

The fit returns $f_r = 7.142\,\text{GHz}$ with FWHM $\Delta f = 1.4\,\text{MHz}$.

Total linewidth: $\kappa/2\pi = 1.4\,\text{MHz}$.

Loaded Q: $Q_L = f_r/\Delta f \approx 5.1\times 10^{3}$.

The depth of the dip lets us split $\kappa_{\text{ext}}$ from $\kappa_{\text{int}}$; typically we design for $\kappa_{\text{ext}}\!\approx\!\kappa_{\text{int}}$ (critical coupling) for fast readout without sacrificing photons to loss.

Going further: punch-out

Repeat the sweep while ramping the probe power up. At low power the resonator sits at its dressed frequency $\omega_r + \chi\langle\sigma_z\rangle$; at high power photon number drives the qubit into the highly excited manifold and the resonator pops back to its bare frequency. The discontinuity is exactly the dispersive shift $\chi$, and tells you the qubit is alive on the chip.

STEP 02

Two-Tone Qubit Spectroscopy

Now we hunt for the qubit. Park a probe at the resonator and watch its phase or amplitude while a second tone scans through GHz looking for the frequency that excites $|0\rangle \to |1\rangle$.

Procedure

  1. Park the readout LO at the dressed resonator frequency found in Step 1.
  2. Apply a long, low-power spectroscopy pulse from the drive AWG and sweep its carrier frequency $f_d$ over a few hundred MHz (an educated guess from design is usually right within ~100 MHz).
  3. For every $f_d$, measure the resonator response. When $f_d = f_q$, the qubit gets pumped into a mixed state, the average $\langle\sigma_z\rangle$ changes, and the dispersive shift $\chi\sigma_z$ moves the resonator → the readout signal changes.
$$ \omega_r^{\text{dressed}} \;=\; \omega_r + \chi\,\langle\sigma_z\rangle, \qquad \chi \;\approx\; \frac{g^2}{\Delta}\,\frac{\alpha}{\Delta + \alpha}. $$
where $g$ is the qubit–resonator coupling, $\Delta = \omega_q - \omega_r$ is the qubit–resonator detuning, and $\alpha$ is the transmon anharmonicity. The qubit response is a Lorentzian peak / dip in the readout signal centred at $\omega_q$:
$$ P_e(\omega_d) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\Omega^2\, T_1 T_2}{1 + (\omega_d - \omega_q)^2 T_2^2 + \Omega^2\, T_1 T_2}. $$
Fig. 2.1 — readout signal vs drive frequency
Worked example

Lorentzian fit returns the centre $f_q = 5.213\,\text{GHz}$ with a power-broadened FWHM of $\sim 2\,\text{MHz}$.

Take the drive power down by 6 dB and repeat: the line should narrow toward the intrinsic linewidth $\Gamma_2/2\pi = 1/(\pi T_2)$. The frequency at zero power (extrapolated) is the real qubit frequency.

If a second, weaker peak appears at $f_q + \alpha/2$, that's the two-photon $|0\rangle\!\to\!|2\rangle$ transition — its position gives the anharmonicity, here $\alpha/2\pi \approx -220\,\text{MHz}$.

Tip

Start broad and ugly. A 500 MHz scan in 1 MHz steps with a long (≥ 2 µs) high-power saturation pulse will reveal almost anything within ±250 MHz of the design frequency. Only once you've found a feature do you tighten the span and lower the power.

STEP 03

Rabi Oscillations & the π Pulse

A coherent drive at $f_q$ rotates the Bloch vector. By varying the pulse duration (or amplitude) and reading out, we calibrate the area that flips the qubit from $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$: the π pulse.

Procedure

  1. Tune the drive LO so that the up-converted carrier equals $f_q$.
  2. Send a square (or Gaussian) drive pulse of duration $\tau$ at fixed amplitude $A$, then immediately read out.
  3. Sweep $\tau$ from 0 to a few hundred ns. The excited-state population oscillates as $\sin^2(\Omega_R \tau /2)$.
  4. Fit a damped cosine and read off the Rabi frequency $\Omega_R$.
$$ P_{|1\rangle}(\tau) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(1 \;-\; \cos(\Omega_R\,\tau)\;e^{-\tau/T_{\text{Rabi}}}\Bigr). $$
The π-pulse duration is
$$ t_\pi \;=\; \frac{\pi}{\Omega_R} \;=\; \frac{T_{\text{Rabi-period}}}{2}. $$
Equivalently, fixing $\tau$ and sweeping amplitude gives a "power Rabi" that calibrates the AWG gain for a π rotation.
Fig. 3.1 — |1⟩ population vs pulse length
Worked example

Fit returns $\Omega_R/2\pi = 12.5\,\text{MHz}$, envelope decay $T_{\text{Rabi}} = 1.8\,\mu s$.

Therefore

$t_\pi = 1/(2\,f_R) = 1/(2\times 12.5\,\text{MHz}) = 40\,\text{ns}$.

A π/2 pulse is half of that: $t_{\pi/2} = 20\,\text{ns}$.

For all-Gaussian pulses we usually use a "power Rabi" with fixed σ (say 10 ns total length) and find the amplitude that produces the first maximum.

What can go wrong

  • If $f_d \neq f_q$ exactly, the visible Rabi frequency is $\sqrt{\Omega_R^2 + \Delta^2}$ and the contrast drops — useful diagnostic.
  • Leakage into $|2\rangle$ shows up as a "beat" on top of the Rabi: cure with DRAG-shaped pulses.
STEP 04

Energy Relaxation Time T₁

Prepare $|1\rangle$, wait a variable time, read out. The probability of finding the qubit still excited falls exponentially with characteristic time $T_1$.

Procedure

  1. Apply a calibrated π pulse.
  2. Wait a delay $\tau$ (idle).
  3. Read out.
  4. Sweep $\tau$ from 0 to ~5×T₁ (e.g. 0 → 200 µs). Average ≥ 1k shots per point.
$$ P_{|1\rangle}(\tau) \;=\; e^{-\tau/T_1}. $$
$T_1$ is set by spontaneous emission into the environment (mostly the Purcell channel through the readout resonator, plus dielectric loss on the chip).
$$ \frac{1}{T_1^{\text{Purcell}}} \;=\; \Bigl(\frac{g}{\Delta}\Bigr)^2 \kappa. $$
Fig. 4.1 — excited-state decay
Worked example

Exponential fit: $T_1 = 42\,\mu s$.

Cross-check against Purcell: with $g/2\pi = 60\,\text{MHz}$, $\Delta/2\pi = (7.142 - 5.213) = 1.929\,\text{GHz}$, $\kappa/2\pi = 1.4\,\text{MHz}$:

$T_1^{\text{Purcell}} = (\Delta/g)^2/\kappa = (1929/60)^2/(2\pi\!\times\!1.4\,\text{MHz}) \approx 117\,\mu s$.

The measured T₁ is well below the Purcell limit, so we are dominated by other loss (probably TLS / dielectric). That's where you point your engineering effort next.

Sanity checks

  • Measure T₁ many times and histogram it — fluctuations of 20–30% over hours are normal and reveal sparse TLS defects.
  • Make sure your delay sweep is much longer than your largest expected T₁, or you'll fit a misleadingly short value from the upper portion of the curve.
STEP 05

Ramsey Interference & T₂*

Two π/2 pulses separated by a free-evolution time $\tau$. Any detuning $\delta = f_d - f_q$ imprints a phase, so the readout oscillates in $\tau$ at $\delta$; the envelope decays at $T_2^*$, the dephasing time set by quasi-static noise.

Procedure

  1. Detune the drive deliberately by a known amount (e.g. $\delta = 500\,\text{kHz}$) so you get clean fringes.
  2. Sequence: π/2 — wait τ — π/2 — read. Sweep $\tau$.
  3. Fit a damped cosine: $a\cos(2\pi\delta\,\tau + \phi)\,e^{-(\tau/T_2^*)^n}$ with $n\in[1,2]$ depending on noise spectrum.
  4. The fitted frequency $\delta_{\text{fit}}$ tells you how far off $f_q$ you actually are; iterate to land on resonance.
$$ P_{|1\rangle}(\tau) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl[1 + \cos(2\pi\delta\,\tau)\, e^{-\tau/T_2^*}\Bigr]. $$
The dephasing rate decomposes as
$$ \frac{1}{T_2^*} \;=\; \frac{1}{2T_1} \;+\; \frac{1}{T_\varphi^*}, $$
where $T_\varphi^*$ is the pure dephasing time from low-frequency frequency fluctuations (charge / flux / TLS).
Fig. 5.1 — Ramsey fringes
Worked example

Fit: $\delta_{\text{fit}} = 0.50\,\text{MHz}$, $T_2^* = 28\,\mu s$.

If we set $\delta_{\text{set}} = 0.50\,\text{MHz}$ the qubit is on resonance. If we instead set it to 0.40 MHz, our LO frequency is high by 100 kHz; subtract that off and re-sweep.

With $T_1 = 42\,\mu s$ the pure dephasing time is

$1/T_\varphi^* = 1/T_2^* - 1/(2T_1) \Rightarrow T_\varphi^* \approx 42\,\mu s$.

Why two purposes

Ramsey is simultaneously the most accurate frequency-finder we have and the most direct measure of slow dephasing. Run it overnight and you can plot the qubit's frequency vs. time — that drift is your dephasing budget.

STEP 06

Hahn Echo & T₂

The Hahn-echo sequence inserts a π pulse halfway through the Ramsey wait, which refocuses any quasi-static frequency offset. What's left is the dephasing from noise faster than the echo time — usually a much smaller effect.

Procedure

  1. Sequence: π/2 — τ/2 — π — τ/2 — π/2 — read.
  2. Sweep total wait $\tau$. There are no fringes now — just a decay.
  3. Fit a single exponential (or stretched exponential) to extract $T_2^{\text{echo}}$.
$$ P_{|1\rangle}(\tau) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[1 + e^{-\tau/T_2^{\text{echo}}}\bigr]. $$
The echo suppresses noise at frequencies below $\sim 1/\tau$, so the inferred coherence time obeys
$$ T_2^{\text{echo}} \;\geq\; T_2^*, \qquad \text{and}\qquad T_2^{\text{echo}} \leq 2 T_1. $$
The bound $2T_1$ is the absolute fundamental ceiling on phase coherence.
Fig. 6.1 — echo decay
Worked example

Fit: $T_2^{\text{echo}} = 58\,\mu s$.

Compare to $2T_1 = 84\,\mu s$: we are at $\sim 0.69\,(2T_1)$, so there's still a non-trivial pure-dephasing contribution from high-frequency noise that echo can't refocus.

Compare to $T_2^* = 28\,\mu s$: the echo bought us a factor of ~2, telling us that quasi-static drift (slow flux/charge noise) was a big part of the Ramsey decay.

What to do next

  • CPMG / dynamical decoupling. Replace the single π with a train of N π pulses to suppress noise up to higher frequencies.
  • Noise spectroscopy. Sweep N and reconstruct the dephasing spectrum $S_\varphi(\omega)$.
